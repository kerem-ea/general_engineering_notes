# Valence Bond Theory and Sigma/Pi Bonds

**Valence Bond (VB) theory** describes a covalent bond as the overlap of two half-filled atomic orbitals (one from each bonding atom), which allows the two electrons to be shared and stabilizes the system.

A bond forms when:
1. An orbital on one atom overlaps an orbital on another atom.
2. The two single electrons combine into a shared pair.

The strength of a bond increases with the degree of orbital overlap.

## Sigma ($\sigma$) Bonds

A **sigma bond** is formed by the **end-to-end** overlap of orbitals along the internuclear axis. Electron density is concentrated along the axis between the nuclei.

Sources of $\sigma$ bonds:
- $s$-$s$ overlap (e.g. $\text{H}_2$)
- $s$-$p$ overlap (e.g. $\text{HCl}$)
- $p$-$p$ end-to-end overlap (e.g. $\text{Cl}_2$)

All single bonds in Lewis structures are $\sigma$ bonds.

## Pi ($\pi$) Bonds

A **pi bond** is formed by the **side-by-side** overlap of two $p$ orbitals. Electron density lies in two lobes above and below the internuclear axis. There is a node (zero electron density) along the internuclear axis itself.

- Every double bond consists of one $\sigma$ + one $\pi$ bond.
- Every triple bond consists of one $\sigma$ + two $\pi$ bonds.
- There can only be one $\sigma$ bond between any two atoms; additional bonds are always $\pi$ bonds.

## Relative Strengths

$\sigma$ bonds are generally stronger than $\pi$ bonds because end-to-end overlap is more extensive than side-by-side overlap.

| Bond | $\sigma$ contribution | Additional $\pi$ contribution |
|---|---|---|
| $\mathrm{C-C}\text{ single}$ | $347\text{ kJ/mol}$ | None |
| $\mathrm{C=C}\text{ double}$ | $347\text{ kJ/mol}$ | $+267\text{ kJ/mol}$ |
| $\mathrm{C\equiv C}\text{ triple}$ | $347\text{ kJ/mol}$ | $+267 + 225\text{ kJ/mol}$ |

## Rotation

- Free rotation about single ($\sigma$) bonds is possible because end-to-end overlap is symmetric around the internuclear axis.
- Rotation about double or triple bonds is strongly restricted because it would break the side-by-side $\pi$ overlap.
