# Hydrocarbons and Isomerism

(OpenStax Chemistry 2e, section 20.1, pages 959–971)

Hydrocarbons are organic compounds composed entirely of carbon and hydrogen atoms. Carbon's ability to form four stable covalent bonds and catenate into chains and rings yields immense structural diversity.

## Classification of Aliphatic Hydrocarbons

| Family | Saturation | Carbon Hybridization | General Formula (Acyclic) | Characteristic Bond | Suffix |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Alkanes** | Saturated | $sp^3$ (tetrahedral, $109.5^\circ$) | $\mathrm{C}_n\mathrm{H}_{2n+2}$ | All single $\mathrm{C}-\mathrm{C}$ ($\sigma$ bonds) | *-ane* |
| **Alkenes** | Unsaturated | $sp^2$ (trigonal planar, $120^\circ$) | $\mathrm{C}_n\mathrm{H}_{2n}$ | One or more double $\mathrm{C}=\mathrm{C}$ ($1\sigma + 1\pi$) | *-ene* |
| **Alkynes** | Unsaturated | $sp$ (linear, $180^\circ$) | $\mathrm{C}_n\mathrm{H}_{2n-2}$ | One or more triple $\mathrm{C}\equiv\mathrm{C}$ ($1\sigma + 2\pi$) | *-yne* |

### Cyclic Hydrocarbons

Cycloalkanes contain closed rings of carbon atoms with the general formula $\mathrm{C}_n\mathrm{H}_{2n}$ (e.g. cyclopropane $\mathrm{C_3H_6}$, cyclohexane $\mathrm{C_6H_{12}}$). Cyclohexane adopts a puckered "chair" conformation that relieves ring strain and eclipsing interactions, maintaining bond angles close to $109.5^\circ$.

## Types of Isomerism

Isomers are distinct chemical species sharing the same molecular formula.

### 1. Constitutional (Structural) Isomers

Molecules with identical molecular formulas but different bonding connectivities of their atoms. For example, butane ($\mathrm{C_4H_{10}}$) possesses two constitutional isomers:
- **Normal butane ($n$-butane):** straight chain, $\mathrm{CH_3-CH_2-CH_2-CH_3}$ (boiling point $-0.5^\circ\text{C}$).
- **Isobutane (2-methylpropane):** branched chain, $\mathrm{CH_3-CH(CH_3)-CH_3}$ (boiling point $-11.7^\circ\text{C}$).

Branching reduces external molecular surface area, weakening intermolecular dispersion forces and lowering the boiling point (see [[chem/lecture-01/intermolecular-forces|Intermolecular Forces]]).

### 2. Geometric (Stereo) Isomers: Cis and Trans

Free rotation occurs readily around single $\sigma$ bonds in alkanes. In contrast, the $\pi$ bond in alkenes prevents free rotation around the $\mathrm{C}=\mathrm{C}$ double bond without breaking the $\pi$ orbital overlap.

When each double-bonded carbon bears two different substituents, two geometric stereoisomers arise:
- **Cis isomer:** Similar priority groups are positioned on the same side of the double bond.
- **Trans isomer:** Similar priority groups are positioned on opposite sides of the double bond.

Example: 2-butene ($\mathrm{C_4H_8}$):
- *cis*-2-butene has a net dipole moment due to dipole alignment (see [[chem/lecture-05/molecular-polarity|Molecular Polarity]]).
- *trans*-2-butene is nonpolar because individual bond dipoles cancel by symmetry.

## Systematic IUPAC Naming Rules

1. Identify the longest continuous chain of carbon atoms to determine the root alkane name (meth-, eth-, prop-, but-, pent-, hex-, hept-, oct-, non-, dec-).
2. Number the carbon chain from the end closest to any double/triple bonds, or closest to substituent alkyl groups if saturated.
3. Identify attached alkyl branches (methyl, ethyl, propyl) and designate their position numbers.
4. Assemble names alphabetically, using prefixes di-, tri-, tetra- for multiple identical substituents (these prefixes do not affect alphabetical ordering).
