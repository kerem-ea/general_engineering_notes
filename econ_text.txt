Cost, price, revenue and profit

The cost of a product is what the producer spends to make it, while the price is what the market pays
for it. The two are determined by different mechanisms and should not be confused: the cost depends
on the technology, fuel, efficiency and financing of the producer, whereas the price is determined by
the market as a whole. In an electricity market, for instance, the price in each hour is set by the most
expensive plant needed to meet the demand (i.e., the marginal plant), and every producer receives the
same price regardless of its own cost. For the sake of simplicity, we are expressing here the market
price as the average value per energy (i.e., MWh), but in some cases the common unit may be different
(e.g., EUR/kg or EUR/L).
Revenue is the money received from sales (see Eq. 1), while profit is what remains after paying
the costs (see Eq. 2). In other words, a high revenue says nothing about the profitability of a project
until the costs are known.

Other allocations are possible (e.g., charging the fixed costs only to the output in peak hours), but
the average-cost allocation is the convention behind the levelized cost presented in Section 7.

2.1

Fixed costs

A fixed cost is a cost whose total amount per year does not depend on how much the plant produces.
It is caused by having the plant, not by running it: once the decision to build is taken, it must be
paid even if the plant stands still for a whole year. Fixed costs are therefore committed in advance
and cannot be avoided in the short term by producing less.
Two consequences follow from this definition. First, a fixed cost is constant in total (e/yr), but
it falls per unit (e/MWh) as the production rises, because the same amount is shared by more MWh
(see Eq. 4). This is why the utilisation of a plant matters so much for capital-heavy technologies.
Second, fixed costs do not influence the short-term decision to produce or not in a given hour, since
they are paid either way. Instead, they matter for the long-term decision of building the plant at all.
Typical fixed costs of an energy plant are: (i) the capital cost (CAPEX), i.e., buying and building the
plant, which is paid once at the start, and (ii) the fixed operation and maintenance (fixed O&M), i.e.,
staff, insurance, grid connection fees and scheduled service, which is paid every year independently of
the output.

2.2

Variable costs

A variable cost is a cost whose total grows in proportion to the amount produced. It is caused by
running the plant: if the plant produces nothing, the variable cost is zero, while if it produces twice as
much, the variable cost doubles. Per unit (e/MWh), the variable cost is therefore roughly constant,
which is the opposite behaviour of a fixed cost.
Because it can be avoided by not producing, the variable cost is what decides whether a plant should
run in a given hour: it is only worth producing when the market price is above the variable cost in
a scenario that products are sold immediately to the market. This is why plants with low variable
costs (e.g., wind and solar) produce first, while plants with high variable costs (e.g., gas peakers) only
produce when prices are high. Typical variable costs are fuel, CO2 allowances, consumables and wear
that scale with the output. Note that the variable cost per unit of output depends on the conversion
efficiency, since more fuel must be bought for each unit of product when the efficiency is low (see Eq.
5).

2.3

Constrained market price and production volume scenario

Profit is what an investor ultimately cares about, but it is hard to calculate. It depends on the market
price, and the price is set outside the system boundary we normally draw around a plant or technology.
It results from the whole market: fuel prices, weather, demand, policy and every other producer. An
engineer can estimate the cost of a wind turbine from its design, but the electricity price it will receive
in fifteen years is much more uncertain.
Therefore, in the rest of these notes we adopt the following frame of reference: we treat the
price and the quantity sold as given so that we can focus on what lies inside our system boundary and
can be estimated with engineering methods, i.e., the costs. Under this assumption, comparing designs
or technologies means comparing their costs, and the best option is the one with the lowest cost per

The time value of money

A euro today is worth more than a euro in ten years, because today’s euro can be invested in the
meantime. The previous sections ignored this effect, but for assets that live 15-30 years it can change
the answer.
Money tied up in a project cannot be used elsewhere. The opportunity cost is the return of the
best alternative that is given up. For instance, investing 3 Me in a battery means not putting those
3 Me into government bonds or another plant.
The interest rate is the price of using money over time: the return a lender or investor expects
for giving up the money now. A value invested today grows over time as described in Eq. 10, a process
called compounding.

4.1

Estimating the discount rate

The discount rate is the minimum return an investor requires from a project. It can be estimated as a
risk-free rate plus a risk premium (see Eq. 11). The risk-free rate is the return on a near-riskless asset,
such as a long-term Danish government bond, while the risk premium is the extra return demanded
for the risks of the project (e.g., technology, prices, regulation and construction). Therefore, the same
cash flows are worth less to an investor when the project is riskier, because they are discounted more
heavily.

Opportunity cost vs. inflation

Opportunity cost and inflation are often confused, but they are different effects. The opportunity cost
reflects that money today can earn a return, which holds even with zero inflation. Inflation, on the
other hand, is the rise of the general price level, so that a euro buys less over time. A nominal rate
includes inflation, while a real rate removes it (see Eq. 12). As a rule, cash flows given in today’s
prices should be discounted with the real rate, while cash flows that include future price increases
should be discounted with the nominal rate. The two should never be mixed.

5.1

Discounted cash flow

The yearly money in minus money out is called cash flow. Drawn on a time axis, it shows the
investment as a large negative bar in year 0 and the operating margins (i.e., revenue minus variable
costs minus fixed O&M) as positive bars afterwards.
The undiscounted cash flow lists the money in and out per year at face value, while the
discounted cash flow multiplies each year by 1/(1 + r)t , so that every entry is in today’s euros and
the entries can be added. The time needed for the cumulative cash flow to become positive is called
the payback time, which can be calculated with undiscounted or discounted values.

7

Key performance indicators: NPV, LCOE and IRR

Three indicators answer three different questions about the same cash flow: how much value is created
(net present value, NPV), what the product costs (levelized cost, LCOE), and what return the project
gives (internal rate of return, IRR). All three are defined from the condition NPV = 0.

Synthesis: comparing the indicators

The three indicators are three views of the same equation (NPV = 0), each solved for a different
unknown. A good project has a positive NPV, an LCOE below the expected price, and an IRR above
the discount rate. For a single project, these three conditions are equivalent. However, each indicator
answers a different question and has its own limitations, as summarised in Table 2.
